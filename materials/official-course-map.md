## Seams (link, never teach)

- **Message structure, the ask, where the ask sits, bad news, disagreement as structure:**
  learn-communication-with-phoebe (sessions 1, 3, 4, 6). Its session 4 has a clarity scorer that
  counts words per sentence, passives, 30 corporate abstractions, dates/numbers and the ASK
  POSITION into a 0-100 rubric. Our bench is distinct: language-level habits of non-native
  writers (hedges, hidden verbs, over-formal stock phrases, regional idioms, politeness markers),
  NO rubric score, and calibrated as a percentile against real business email. We teach "put the

## Verified facts, with tiers

Tier A = read at source in this build. Tier R = reported (secondary, not opened). Cut = not used.

### Plain English Campaign (UK) - Tier A
Source: "How to write in plain English" (free guide PDF, plainenglish.co.uk/free-guides, read
2026-10-06) and "Writing business emails" (same page).
- Definition: plain English "is a message, written with the reader in mind and with the right
  tone of voice, that is clear and concise."
- Myths list: not "cat sat on the mat" writing; does not mean reducing length or changing
  meaning; not about banning new or long words or letting grammar slip; "not as easy as we would
  like to think".
- "Most experts would agree that clear writing should have an average sentence length of 15 to 20
  words." Vary it.
- "aim to make about 80 to 90% of your verbs active." Good uses of passive listed (softening,
  doer unknown, avoiding blame).
- Nominalizations section: "We had a discussion about the matter" -> "We discussed the matter".
- "Use 'you' and 'we'"; "Don't be afraid to give instructions"; on "please": put "please" in front
  of a command, BUT "if something must be done, it is best not to say 'please' as it gives the
  reader the option to refuse the request." (Teach as nuance, session 2.)
- Imagine "talking to your reader across a table" (the across-the-table test, session 1).
- Grammar myths: may start with and/but/because/so/however; split infinitives; end with a
  preposition.
- Words to avoid list includes: additional (extra), advise (tell), commence (start), ensure (make
  sure), forward (send), in accordance with, in excess of (more than), in respect of (for), in the
  event of (if), prior to (before), purchase (buy), regarding (about), should you wish (if you
  want), terminate (end), whilst (while).
- Business email guide: open with "Dear", close with "Best wishes" or "Regards"; "'Hi' is not
  really appropriate for business emails unless you are familiar with the person"; "people are
  unlikely to be offended if you are too formal, but some may think you are being rude if you are
  too informal" (THE DISAGREEMENT, taught session 1 and 2); title your email clearly in the subject
  box, keep titles short; keep to a maximum of five paragraphs; its "how not to" example is
  over-casual and indecisive ("could we, like, arrange something maybe? No rush!").

### US federal plain language guidelines - Tier A
plainlanguage.gov now redirects to digital.gov/guides/plain-language; the original guidelines are
archived in github.com/GSA/plainlanguage.gov, `_pages/guidelines/` (read 2026-10-06).
- Avoid hidden verbs: "A hidden verb (or nominalization) is a verb converted into a noun";
  endings -ment, -tion, -sion, -ance; link verbs achieve, effect, give, have, make, reach, take;
  "Hidden verbs often go hand in hand with passive verbs and combine to give an officious and
  longwinded style."
- Use active voice: "Active voice makes it clear who is supposed to do what." Passive is
  acceptable when the law is the actor or the doer does not matter. Identifying passives: a form of
  "to be" plus a past participle (generally -ed). (This is exactly the detector's heuristic.)
- Write short sentences: "Express only one idea in each sentence." No numeric limit stated on
  these pages. (Do not attribute a word limit to the US guidelines.)
- Use contractions, citing Danielson and Larosa (1989) that they enhance readability: the study
  itself is Tier R (reported via the guideline).
- Use simple words and phrases: long substitution list ("a number of" -> some, "at the present
  time" -> now, "commence" -> begin, "in order to" -> to (digital.gov Style page)).
- digital.gov "Short and simple": cut excess modifiers; avoid doublets.

### Gowers, The Complete Plain Words - Tier A (OCR of the 1954 HMSO edition)
archive.org item in.gov.ignca.28753 (Gowers, Ernest, 1954), djvu text; OCR noisy but legible on
the quoted lines.
- Rule (6): "Use no more words than are necessary to do the job ... Go straight to what you have
  to say, without precautionary words". Quoted on pages as "precautionary words".
- Rule (11): "Do not use what have been called the 'dry meaningless formulae' of commercialese";
  officials "do not write your esteemed letter to hand or address their correspondents as your
  good self", but "foolish begs are common" ("I beg to ...").
- Gowers quotes R. L. Stevenson on writing what you mean (not used on pages).

### CEFR Companion Volume (Council of Europe, 2020) - Tier A
PDF rm.coe.int/16809ea0d4, read 2026-10-06. Page numbers are the printed ones.
- p.24 and p.37: C2 "has no relation whatsoever with what is sometimes referred to as the
  performance of an idealised 'native speaker'"; "native speaker" descriptors changed "because
  this term has become controversial".
- p.133: phonology scale redeveloped: "The focus on accent and on accuracy instead of on
  intelligibility has been detrimental to the development of the teaching of pronunciation."
- p.137 Sociolinguistic appropriateness: B1 "Is aware of the salient politeness conventions and
  acts appropriately"; B2 "Can express themselves confidently, clearly and politely in a formal or
  informal register, appropriate to the situation and person(s) concerned"; C1 "Can adjust their
  level of formality (register and style) to suit the social context" and "Can frame critical
  remarks or express strong disagreement diplomatically."
- p.76 Formal discussion (meetings): B1 "Can put over a point of view clearly, but has difficulty
  engaging in debate"; B2 "Can participate actively in routine and non-routine formal
  discussion"; C1 "Can make critical remarks or express disagreement diplomatically."
- p.88 Turntaking: B1 "Can intervene in a discussion on a familiar topic, using a suitable phrase
  to get the floor"; B2 "Can use stock phrases (e.g. 'That's a difficult question to answer') to
  gain time and keep the turn"; C1 "Can select a suitable phrase from a readily available range of
  discourse functions to preface their remarks appropriately in order to get the floor".
- p.82-83 Correspondence: B1 "Can compose basic e-mails/letters of a factual nature (e.g. to
  request information or to ask for and give confirmation)"; B2 "Can use formality and
  conventions appropriate to the context when writing personal and professional letters and
  e-mails."
- p.65-66 Addressing audiences: B1 "clear enough to be followed without difficulty most of the
  time"; "Can take follow-up questions, but may have to ask for repetition if the delivery is
  rapid"; B2 "clear, systematically developed presentation, with highlighting of significant
  points".

## Bench canon (frozen 2026-10-06; python reference == node on every count)

Base: CMU Enron corpus, sent folders of 146 mailboxes, 125,682 messages scanned, 20,722 eligible
(own text 40-250 words after cutting quoted replies, no HTML, personal and profane mail
dropped, duplicates removed), 400 drawn with random.Random(20261006). Masked: every 6+ digit
run, phone-shaped numbers, every token with "@". 189,984 bytes. Writers: mostly native speakers
in one US energy company, 2000-2001. A picture of real email, never a standard of good email.

Cross-check (materials/crosscheck.py, output in materials/bench-canon.txt): Python and node
agree on all 400 x 10 counts, on 400 shuffled re-runs, on every ladder rung and on the break
totals. RESULT AGREE.

Calibration:
- 2,720 sentences in the 400 emails; length p50 10, p75 17, p90 24, p95 29 words; mean 12.1.
  LONG_LIMIT = 30, i.e. longer than about 19 in 20 real sentences: 109 of 2,720 (4.0 percent)
  exceed it.
- Flags per 100 words across the 400: median 1.11, 90th percentile 3.03, pooled 1.41.
  168 of 400 (42 percent) have zero flags.
- Share of the 400 with at least one: hedge 18.5%, hidden verb 6.0%, passive 35.25% (widget
  prints 35.3%), long sentence 22.0%, over-formal 5.25% (prints 5.3%), regional 0.0%, politeness
  marker 50.5%, any flag 58%.
- Totals: 32,944 words; hedge 94, hidden verb 27, passive 209, long 109, over-formal 24,
  regional 0, politeness 328, flags 463.
- Most frequent real hedges: "i think" 32, "i believe" 13, "i'm not sure" 9, "maybe" 9.
  Most frequent over-formal: "pursuant to" 6, "prior to" 5, "do not hesitate to" 5.
  Regional idioms: zero in the base (a US company); the flag is a reader-risk flag that the base
  cannot calibrate, and the page says so.
- Median email length 68 words.

Ladder (constructed, Mei Lin Goh), counts H/N/P/L/F/R, politeness, flags, per 100 words,
real emails below it:

The anti-lever (formalise the plain final) raises flags 0 -> 24, words 55 -> 95, and drops
politeness markers 2 -> 0: more formal is not more polite on the measured features.
Longest sentence in the draft: 53 words.

Break button: shuffle word order (Mulberry32, seed 7 for the box, 1000+i per sample email).
Ladder flags after shuffling: 22->9, 15->3, 7->2, 3->2, 1->1, 0->0, anti 24->6 (single words such
as "maybe", "kindly", "herewith" survive; phrases do not). Over the 400 emails: phrase-detector
hits 354 -> 73; multi-word hits 337 -> 56, of which 43 are chance be+participle passives (the
loosest pattern). Word counts unchanged by construction (32,944).

## Not covered (honest list)
