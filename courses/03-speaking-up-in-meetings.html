Learn Business English with Phoebe · Session 3 of 6

Speaking up in meetings

In writing you can take your time. In a meeting the turn opens for a moment and then it is gone, and the person with the best answer is often the one still building the perfect sentence. Tonight is the spoken English of meetings: short phrases that take the floor, a four-part way to disagree that stays polite without going vague, and what to say when someone talks over you or when you did not catch what they said. You leave with three lines written for your next meeting and said aloud until they come out without thinking.

Part 0

Where we are

Sessions 1 and 2 were about writing, where the reader waits for you. A meeting does not wait. Mei Lin Goh, a constructed data analyst at Harbourline, a constructed logistics company in Singapore, sits in a weekly video call with eight colleagues from three offices. The question on the table is why customers left in Q3. She has the answer in a chart on her second screen. The call moves from churn to budget, and she never says a word. Afterwards a colleague says "you should have jumped in". Her English was never the problem. She did not have the words ready that get a person into a conversation, so she waited for a silence that never came.

Part 1 · covers joining the conversation

A turn opens for a moment

6 min live

In a lively meeting nobody hands out turns. One speaker slows down, there is a short gap, and whoever speaks first gets the floor. The gap is too short to build a sentence in. What fits in it is a short, ready-made phrase that says "I am coming in", and once you have said it, people wait for the rest.

LivePhrases that take the floor3 min▶

An entry phrase does two jobs. It claims the turn, and it tells the room what kind of turn it is, so people know whether to wait for a question, an addition or a challenge. Learn three, not thirty. Under pressure you will reach for whatever you have said most often.

PhraseWhat it signalsWhen to use it
"Can I add something here?"An addition, not a challengeAlmost anywhere; the safest all-purpose entry
"Building on what Priya said, ..."You agree and extendWhen your point connects to the last speaker's
"Before we move on, one point on churn."The topic is about to closeWhen you see the agenda moving past your point
"Could I come back to the Q3 numbers?"You want an earlier topic reopenedWhen the moment passed and you still need it
"Quick question on that."A short question, not a speechWhen you need one fact before you can contribute

Notice that none of them apologises. "Sorry, maybe this is not relevant, but" spends the gap on doubt and tells the room it can stop listening. The entry phrase is polite because it asks ("Can I...?") or names a link ("Building on..."), not because it apologises.

LivePhrases that buy thinking time2 min▶

Getting the floor is half of it. The other half is keeping it while your brain catches up with your mouth. Fluent speakers do this all the time, with short stock phrases that fill the space without sounding lost:

★ Time-buyers, said calmly"That's a difficult question to answer. Let me think about it for a second." "Good question. The short answer is ..." "Let me put it another way." "There are two parts to this. The first is ..." "Where was I? Yes, the delivery numbers."

"There are two parts to this" is the most useful of these. It buys time, it tells the listener how long you will talk, and it gives your answer a shape before you have found the words.

Self-studyWhat the CEFR expects at each level3 min▶

The Council of Europe's framework for languages (the CEFR Companion Volume, 2020) has a scale just for turntaking, and its three middle levels describe exactly the progression in this Part:

  • B1: "Can intervene in a discussion on a familiar topic, using a suitable phrase to get the floor".
  • B2: "Can use stock phrases (e.g. 'That's a difficult question to answer') to gain time and keep the turn".
  • C1: "Can select a suitable phrase from a readily available range of discourse functions to preface their remarks appropriately in order to get the floor".

Two things stand out. First, every level is about phrases: the framework treats getting the floor as a matter of having the right short expression ready, which is good news, because phrases can be prepared. Second, the step from B1 to C1 is range and fit, not accent. A C1 speaker has several entry phrases and picks the one that matches the moment.

On accent, the same framework is explicit. It redeveloped its pronunciation scale around intelligibility and says that "the focus on accent and on accuracy instead of on intelligibility has been detrimental to the development of the teaching of pronunciation." The target in a meeting is to be understood the first time, not to sound like someone from somewhere else. How you use your voice, pace and pauses in front of a room is taught in Public Speaking ↗.

Real world

The answer that arrived ten minutes late. A constructed scene: an operations lead in Manila asks on a call why a client's orders dropped. An analyst knows: the client's warehouse moved. She waits for a pause long enough to feel polite, it never comes, and the call moves on to staffing. Ten minutes later she says "Sorry, going back to the earlier point..." and the room has to reload a topic it had closed. "Can I add something here? Their warehouse moved in July." would have taken four seconds at the right moment.

Part 2 · covers disagreeing politely

Disagree in four parts

5 min live

Many professionals who work in a second language either do not disagree at all, or disagree so softly that nobody notices. Both have the same result: the decision goes ahead without your information. A disagreement can be fully polite and still clear, if it has four parts in order: acknowledge, position, reason, offer.

LiveThe four-part move3 min▶

Each part has one job, and each has a short phrase that does it:

PartJobPhrase
AcknowledgeShow you heard the other view"I see why the discount looks attractive."
PositionSay plainly that you differ"I see it differently."
ReasonGive the one fact behind it"because most of the Q3 churn came from late deliveries, not price."
OfferGive the room a next step"Could we test a delivery fix in one region first?"

Put together, Mei Lin's version (constructed) runs: "I see why the discount looks attractive. I see it differently, because most of the Q3 churn came from late deliveries, not price. Could we test a delivery fix in one region first?" Three sentences, none of them long, none of them rude. The acknowledgement is what makes the position safe to say; the offer is what stops it sounding like a block.

Two language notes. "I see it differently" is softer than "I disagree" but just as clear, because nobody can mistake it for agreement. And "because" is the most important word in the move: a position without a reason sounds like a feeling, a position with one sounds like information.

Where the disagreement goes in a longer argument, and how to build a case that changes a decision, is a question of structure, and it is taught in Communication ↗, session 6. Here the job is the sentence you say out loud.

LiveA phrase bank, soft to firm2 min▶

Pick your default from the middle, and know one phrase on each side of it for when the stakes change.

★ Soft to firmsoft "I'm not sure that works for the northern region." "I wonder whether there's another way to look at it." middle "I see it a bit differently, because ..." "I'd question that, because ..." firm "I disagree, because ..." "I don't think that's right. The data shows ..." firmest "I can't support that, because ..."

An honest note about the softest line. The plain-English bench in session 4 counts "I'm not sure" as a hedge, and it is right to: in the 400 real business emails behind that bench, "I'm not sure" is one of the most frequent hedges, turning up 9 times. So why is it in a phrase bank? Because speech and a written request are different situations. One honest softener before a disagreement, said aloud, tells the room you are being careful with a colleague's idea, and your tone and the rest of the sentence carry the point. Six softeners stacked in front of a written request, as in Mei Lin's email in session 1, leave the reader guessing what is being asked. Use one, in speech, on purpose. Do not stack them, and do not let "I'm not sure" be the whole disagreement: follow it with "because".

Self-studyWhat the CEFR says about disagreement2 min▶

The CEFR scale for formal discussion in meetings draws the line exactly where many second-language speakers get stuck. At B1 a speaker "Can put over a point of view clearly, but has difficulty engaging in debate". At B2 they "Can participate actively in routine and non-routine formal discussion". At C1 they "Can make critical remarks or express disagreement diplomatically."

The sociolinguistic scale says something similar at C1: "Can frame critical remarks or express strong disagreement diplomatically." Notice the word in both: diplomatically, not softly. Diplomatic disagreement is still disagreement. The four-part move is one way to do it: the acknowledgement and the offer are the diplomacy, the position and the reason are the disagreement.

Real world

The "yes" that meant no. A constructed scene: a project manager in London asks a team in Singapore, "Can we go live on Monday?" A senior engineer answers "Yes, it may be a bit challenging." The manager hears yes. The engineer meant "no, not safely". On Monday the release fails. The four-part version would have taken one more sentence: "I see why Monday matters. I don't think we can go live safely, because testing finishes Tuesday. Could we go live on Wednesday instead?" Same respect, no room for a wrong reading.

Part 3 · covers interrupting well and being interrupted

Interrupt well, and hold your ground

4 min live

Interrupting is not rude in itself. Interrupting with no signal is. Being interrupted is not a defeat either; most interruptions are questions or corrections, and only a few are takeovers. The skill is telling them apart in a second and having the phrase for each one ready.

LiveInterrupting politely2 min▶

A polite interruption has a signal, a reason and brevity. The signal tells the speaker you know you are cutting in; the reason tells them why it cannot wait; brevity gives the turn back quickly.

★ Coming in on someone"Sorry to jump in, but that number is from June, not September." "Can I come in on that? We saw the same thing in Jakarta." "Just a quick point before you go on: the client moved their warehouse." "Sorry, can I stop you there? I think we're looking at different files."

"Sorry to jump in" is one of the few places where "sorry" earns its place: it is short, it names what you are doing, and it is followed straight away by the content. Compare "Sorry, sorry, maybe I am wrong, but I just wanted to say", which spends the interruption apologising and never reaches the point.

LiveHolding the floor, and asking for repetition2 min▶

When someone takes over, one calm sentence is enough: "If I could just finish this point, then I'd like to hear yours." It is polite, because it promises them a turn. It is firm, because it does not ask permission. Say it once, at normal speed, and keep going. If you need it twice in the same meeting, the problem is no longer language, and it belongs with the person running the meeting.

Asking for repetition is a skill, not a failure. Native speakers ask all the time; they just do it without apologising. Three phrases cover nearly every case:

★ Clarifying, without apology"Could you say that again? The line cut out." "Sorry, could you go through the last number once more?" "Just to check I've understood, you mean the budget decision is next week, not Friday?"

The third is the strongest, because it does not just ask for a repeat. It shows what you understood, so the speaker can correct exactly the part you missed, and the whole room benefits: if you did not catch it, someone else probably did not either. The CEFR treats this as a normal part of competent speaking: its B1 descriptor for addressing audiences says a speaker "Can take follow-up questions, but may have to ask for repetition if the delivery is rapid". Asking is built into the level, not a sign of falling below it.

One honest note for the bench. Session 4's detectors count "just to check" as a hedge, because in a written request it often is: "just to check whether it might be possible" is packaging. In a meeting, "Just to check I've understood" is followed straight away by your understanding, which is the content. The words are the same; the job they do is different. A count can see the words, and only you can see the job.

Real world

The question everyone wanted to ask. A constructed scene: a finance lead in London runs through a forecast at speed. A colleague in Manila says "Just to check I've understood, you mean the forecast assumes the new depot opens in March?" The finance lead says "No, June, sorry, that changes things," and three other people on the call unmute to say they had assumed March too. The clarifying question was not a confession of weak English. It was the most useful thing said in the meeting.

Build-along

Write your three lines, then say them

★ 22 min · everyone builds

A phrase you have only read is a phrase you will not find at speed. A phrase you have written for a specific meeting and said aloud three times is one your mouth already knows. Tonight you prepare one card for one real meeting this week: an entry line, a disagreement in four parts, and a clarifying question. Mei Lin's card comes first, so you can see the shape.

Read Mei Lin's three lines (constructed). Notice how short each one is, and that each could be said in one breath. She wrote them for next week's churn call, with the actual topic and the actual colleague in them, not as general phrases.

★ Step 1 · Mei Lin's card, constructedENTRY Can I add something on churn before we move on? DISAGREE I see why the discount looks attractive. I see it differently, because most of the churn came from late deliveries, not price. Could we test a delivery fix in one region first? CLARIFY Just to check I have understood, you mean the budget decision is next week, not this Friday?

Pick your meeting, and write your entry line. Choose a real meeting in the next seven days. Name the topic you want to speak on. Start from one of the entry phrases in Part 1 and put your actual topic into it.

★ Step 2 · entry line templateMeeting: ______________________ (day, who is in it) The point I want to make: ______________________ Pick one frame: "Can I add something on ________ ?" "Building on what ________ said, ________ ." "Before we move on, one point on ________ ."

Write one disagreement in four parts. Think of the view in that meeting you are most likely to disagree with. Fill the four slots. Keep each part to one short sentence, and keep "because" in the reason.

★ Step 3 · the four-part templateAcknowledge "I see why ______________ ." Position "I see it differently," (or pick from the bank below) Reason "because ______________ ." Offer "Could we ______________ ?" Bank, soft to firm: I'm not sure that works ... -> I see it a bit differently ... -> I'd question that ... -> I disagree ... -> I can't support that ...

Write one clarifying question, then say all three aloud three times. Pick the part of the meeting where you are most likely to lose the thread: a number, a date, a name. Write the "Just to check" line for it. Then stand up, and say each of your three lines aloud, three times, at normal speed. The third time is usually the first one that sounds like you.

★ Step 4 · clarify, then rehearse"Just to check I've understood, you mean ______________ ?" Aloud, three times each: entry [ ] [ ] [ ] disagree [ ] [ ] [ ] clarify [ ] [ ] [ ] Which line still felt stiff on the third go? ____________

What the checker says about Mei Lin's three lines. 59 words, five sentences, the longest 17 words, and one flag: "just to check", counted as a hedge. That flag is the honest limit from Part 3 in action. In a written request you should cut it; in a meeting it introduces the content, so keep it. Spoken lines are short by nature, which is why the checker finds so little in them. Paste your own three lines in and see whether yours are as short.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 3 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Mei Lin has a point on churn, and the speaker is slowing down. What gives her the best chance of getting the turn?

The gap is too short for a full sentence but long enough for an entry phrase, and once the phrase is said the room waits for the rest. Waiting for a long silence often means the topic closes first; starting a full explanation late usually collides with the next speaker.

2 · Which disagreement has all four parts: acknowledge, position, reason, offer?

D acknowledges the other view, states a position, gives the reason with "because", and offers a next step. C stops after the position, so it sounds like a feeling rather than information. A never takes a position at all.

3 · A colleague speaks fast and Mei Lin misses a date. What is the most useful thing to say?

Asking for repetition is part of competent speaking: the CEFR's B1 descriptor for addressing audiences includes having to "ask for repetition if the delivery is rapid". Stating what you understood is stronger than a bare "could you repeat", and it often helps others in the room who missed the same thing.
