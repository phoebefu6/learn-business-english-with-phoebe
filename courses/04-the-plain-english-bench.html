The plain-English bench

Sessions 1 to 3 argued that stiff English costs the reader. Tonight the argument becomes a count. Six detectors read any email you paste, in your browser, and set it against 400 real business emails drawn from the public Enron corpus, so "too formal" stops being an opinion and becomes a position on a real distribution. Mei Lin's stiff draft is rewritten one habit at a time while you watch the count fall. Then two tests the bench runs on itself: the obvious upgrade, "sound more formal", which makes every measured habit worse, and a break button that shuffles the word order to prove the detectors read phrases rather than length.

Part 0

Where we start

A bench is a fixed set of rules applied the same way to every text, so that when two drafts give two numbers the only thing that differed was the draft. The rules are the six habits from session 1, written as phrase lists and patterns. The comparison is 400 real emails, written by people at work, which tells you what ordinary business email actually looks like rather than what a style guide wishes it looked like.

Part 1 · covers what the bench measures

Six detectors and a real distribution

6 min live

The bench does four things in order: finds each habit as a phrase, counts the hits, divides by the number of words so a long email is not punished for being long, and places that rate among the same rate for 400 real emails. Every step is string matching in your browser. There is no language model anywhere in it.

LiveThe 400 real emails, and what they are not3 min▶

The Enron corpus is the largest public collection of real workplace email. It was released during the US Federal Energy Regulatory Commission's investigation of the company, and Carnegie Mellon University publishes a cleaned copy for research. For the bench, only sent folders were read, so every message was written by its mailbox owner, and only the text they typed was kept: quoted replies and forwards were cut at the first marker.

From 125,682 sent messages across 146 mailboxes, 20,722 qualified: 40 to 250 words of the writer's own text, no HTML, no obviously personal or profane mail, no duplicates. The bench holds 400 of them, drawn with a fixed seed. Every run of six or more digits, every phone number and every email address is masked.

What they are not: a standard. The writers were mostly native English speakers in one American energy company in 2000 and 2001, writing internally and fast. The distribution tells you what ordinary business email looked like there. It does not tell you what good email is, and the page never treats the median as a target.

LiveThe six detectors, and how they can be wrong3 min▶

Hedge matches 30 listed phrases ("just wanted to", "maybe", "perhaps", "I think", "possibly", "sorry to bother"). Hidden verb matches a light verb such as make, have, take, give or carry out followed by a noun made from a verb ("make a decision", "carry out a review"), or "the ... of" around a -tion, -ment or -ance noun ("the approval of"). Passive matches a form of "be" followed by a past participle, which is exactly the identification rule in the US plain language guidelines; it skips a short list of adjectives such as "interested" and "pleased". Long sentence counts sentences over 30 words. Over-formal matches 38 stock phrases. Regional idiom matches 17 phrases such as "do the needful" and "revert back".

They are wrong in known ways. The passive detector misses "got" passives and flags the occasional adjective. The hedge list has no entry for "I would imagine". A hidden verb inside an unusual phrase slips through. That is why every hit is listed by name on the widget: you can see each one and overrule it. A detector you can audit is more useful than one you have to trust.

Politeness markers ("please", "thanks", "could you", "would you", "appreciate") are counted and shown, and never added to the flags. On the 400 real emails, 50.5 percent contain at least one.

Self-studyWhere the 30-word limit comes from2 min▶

The Plain English Campaign recommends an average sentence length of 15 to 20 words. An average is not a limit, so the bench needed its own line, and it was calibrated on the base rather than chosen by taste. The 400 emails hold 2,720 sentences. Half are 10 words or fewer; the 90th percentile is 24 words and the 95th is 29. The bench flags sentences over 30 words, which is longer than about 19 in 20 real sentences: 109 of the 2,720 exceed it.

The mean sentence in the base is 12.1 words, well inside the campaign's range. Real email is choppier than formal prose, which is one more reason stiff writing stands out in an inbox.

Read it in this order. The verdict, then flags per 100 words against the real median, then the detector that holds most of your hits, then the named hits underneath. The named hits are where you learn something: the count tells you that, the list tells you what.

LiveFinding 1: the politeness never moved2 min▶

Read the Polite column. Mei Lin's draft has two politeness markers, and so does every rung down to the plain final version. Five rewrites removed 64 words and 22 flags and did not remove one "please" or "thanks". Cutting stiffness and cutting courtesy are different edits. Most people who are afraid of sounding rude are imagining the second when they are being asked for the first.

LiveFinding 2: zero is not the goal, and real writers hedge too2 min▶

The final rung scores 0.00, and 168 of the 400 real emails (42 percent) also score zero. But 58 percent of real emails carry at least one flag, and the commonest real hedge is "I think", 32 times in 400 emails. One hedge where you are genuinely unsure is honest writing. Six stacked in front of one request, as in the draft, is the habit. The bench reads density, not presence, which is why the number is per 100 words.

Likewise for passives: 35.3 percent of real emails contain one, and many are fine. "The slides are attached" is a passive nobody should rewrite.

Self-studyWhy regional idioms read zero in the base2 min▶

Not one of the 400 real emails contains a phrase from the regional list. That is not evidence that the phrases are rare in business English worldwide; it is evidence that this base came from one American company. The base cannot calibrate the regional detector at all, and the bench does not pretend to. The flag exists for a different reason: it tells you that a reader in another office may not know the phrase. Whether to keep it depends on who reads the email, and only you know that.

Part 3 · covers the anti-lever and the break button

Two tests the bench runs on itself

4 min live

A bench that can only report good news measures nothing. These two buttons exist to make the numbers go the wrong way: one with the advice people most often give non-native writers, one by destroying the thing the detectors claim to read.

LiveThe anti-lever: "sound more formal"2 min▶

"Make it sound more professional" usually means "make it more formal", and it is the advice non-native writers hear most. The anti-lever takes Mei Lin's plain final email and formalises it the way a cautious writer would: "We wish to inform you", "it is requested that a meeting be arranged", "kindly revert with your comments in due course", "Thanking you".

The bench measures what happened. Words rose from 55 to 95. Flags rose from 0 to 24: eight passives, eleven over-formal phrases, three hidden verbs, a long sentence and a regional idiom. Its 25.26 flags per 100 words sits above all 400 real emails, and above Mei Lin's original draft. And the two politeness markers went to zero. Nothing in it is less correct; everything in it is further from the reader.

The honest nuance: register is still a choice. A formal greeting to a regulator or a first contact is reasonable, as session 1 showed. What the anti-lever shows is that formality applied inside the sentences costs every habit the bench can see, and buys no measured politeness at all.

LiveThe break button: shuffled words, same length2 min▶

A suspicious reader should ask whether the bench is just measuring length, since stiff emails are long. The break button answers it. It shuffles the order of the words, with a fixed seed, and runs the same detectors again. The words are identical, so anything that only measured length would not move.

On Mei Lin's draft, flags fall from 22 to 9. What survives is mostly single words such as "maybe", "kindly" and "herewith", which are still words after a shuffle, plus sentence lengths that change because the full stops moved. Across all 400 real emails, multi-word phrase hits fall from 337 to 56, and the word count stays at 32,944. Of the 56 that survive, 43 are a form of "be" landing next to some -ed word by chance: the passive pattern is the loosest of the six, and the break button shows exactly that. The detectors read phrases, and you now know which one reads them most loosely.

Real world

"Can you make this more professional?" A manager returns a draft with that note. The writer adds "kindly", "please be informed" and "at your earliest convenience" and sends it back. The manager sends it back again. Both were trying to help. "More professional" usually meant "clearer and surer", and the writer heard "more formal". Run both versions through the bench before the third round: the count settles which way the edit went.

Homework

Before session 5

30 min
  1. Run five of your sent emails through the bench and write down each one's flags per 100 words and its "real emails lower" count. Your median across the five is your baseline for session 6.
  2. Find your top detector. Across the five, which detector holds most of your hits? Rewrite one email fixing only that habit and re-run it.
  3. Overrule the bench once. Find a hit you think is fine, a passive where the doer does not matter or a hedge where you really are unsure, and write one line on why you are keeping it.
  4. Run the break button on your own email. Note which of your hits survive the shuffle. Those are single words, and single words are the easiest habits to drop.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 4 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · The anti-lever formalises Mei Lin's plain email. What does the bench measure?

Formality inside the sentences adds passives, stock phrases and hidden verbs together, and the version measured here lost both politeness markers. Formal and polite are different properties.

2 · Why does the break button shuffle the word order instead of, say, deleting words?

A good break test changes exactly the property the measurement claims to read and nothing else. Shuffling holds length constant, so a fall in hits can only come from the phrases.

3 · Your email scores 0.40 flags per 100 words, below the real median of 1.11. What can you conclude?

The bench gives a position on six habits, not a grade. Whether the email has a clear request in the right place is a structure question, measured next door in Communication's clarity scorer.
