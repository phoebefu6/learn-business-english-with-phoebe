Emails that get answered

An email that needs an answer is competing with every other email in the reader's inbox, and the reader decides which ones to open from a list of subject lines and a line of preview. Part 1 · covers subject lines

A subject line says what the email is for

6 min live

Before anyone reads your email, they read the list it sits in. A subject line is the only part of your message that every recipient is guaranteed to see, and it is read in the same glance as a dozen others. Its job is not to describe the email. Its job is to tell the reader what you need from them, and by when.

LiveAction, topic, date3 min▶

A subject line that gets opened answers the reader's first question, "what do you need from me?", before they click. The shape that does it has three parts, in this order:

Action (what the reader has to do): approval needed, decision needed, please review, for information only. Topic (what it is about, specific enough to find again next month): Q3 retention budget, not "budget". Date (when): by Thu, by 30 Sep, today.

★ Three subject lines with all three parts · constructedApproval needed by Thu: Q3 retention budget Decision needed by Fri: courier contract renewal For information, no action: Q3 churn slides

The third one is worth noticing. "No action" is information too. A reader who knows an email needs nothing from them can file it in a second, and they will trust your next subject line more because this one told the truth.

The UK Plain English Campaign's guide to business email gives the same advice in two short instructions: title your email clearly in the subject box, and keep titles short. Short and clear pull against each other a little. When they do, keep the action and the date and shorten the topic.

LiveWords that waste a subject line2 min▶

Four subject lines turn up in almost every inbox, and each one spends the most valuable space in the email on a word that tells the reader nothing they can act on:

SubjectWhat the reader learnsTry instead
UpdateSomething changed, somewhere"Q3 churn analysis done, slides attached, no action"
Following upYou wrote before, and you are waitingRepeat the original action and date: "Approval needed by Thu: Q3 retention budget"
RequestYou want something, but not whatName the action: "Please review by Fri: courier contract draft"
HiNothing; it is a greeting in the wrong placePut the greeting in the body, the purpose in the subject

"Following up" deserves a second look. When you chase a reply, the reader has already not acted on your first email. A subject line that only says you are chasing gives them no more reason to act than the first one did. Restating the action and the date does.

Self-studyWhen the topic changes, change the subject line2 min▶

Long threads drift. A thread titled "Q3 churn slides" becomes, twelve replies later, a discussion about the retention budget, and the person who needs to approve the budget never opens it because the subject says slides. If the action in a thread changes, edit the subject line when you reply: "Approval needed by Thu: Q3 retention budget (was: Q3 churn slides)". Keeping the old subject in brackets lets people still find the thread.

The same applies to forwarding. "FW: FW: RE: Update" tells the new reader nothing. Replace it with what you need from them.

Real world

The approval that sat in a thread called "slides". An analyst sends her quarterly slides with the subject "Q3 deck". Two days later, in the same thread, she adds "also, could you approve the budget change on slide 9 by Thursday?" Her finance lead, scanning the inbox on Thursday morning, sees "RE: Q3 deck", assumes it is comments on the slides, and leaves it for the weekend. Nothing in the email was wrong. The request was simply invisible from the list. A new subject line, "Approval needed by Thu: budget change on slide 9", would have put it in the pile that gets opened.

Part 2 · covers the request in the first two lines

Put the request where the reader starts reading

5 min live

Once the email is open, the first line does the same job the subject line did: it tells the reader what you need. Many second-language writers were taught to open with a polite paragraph and arrive at the request at the end. In English business email, the polite paragraph is the part that gets skimmed, and the request gets skimmed with it. This Part is about the language of the first line. Where each piece of a message goes, and why, is message structure, taught properly in Communication ↗.

LiveOpeners that delay, and a first line that asks3 min▶

Three opening phrases are worth retiring from emails that need an answer. Each was learned as politeness. Each costs the reader a line before they find out what you want.

OpenerWhat it does to the reader
"I hope this email finds you well."A ritual line the reader skips. Harmless once; in the first line of a request it pushes the request down.
"I am writing to inform you that ..."Announces that information is coming instead of giving it. The reader can see you are writing.
"As per my last email ..."Sends the reader back to an older message, and many readers hear it as a reproach. If the earlier point matters, restate it in one line.

The alternative is a first line that is the request, written as a question, with a date in it:

★ First lines that ask · constructedCould you approve the Q3 retention budget by Thursday? Could you confirm by Friday that the courier rates in the attached file are correct? Can you send me the October volume forecast by 12 noon tomorrow?

A question with "could you" keeps the courtesy. The date tells the reader the request is real. Everything after the first line is context, and context is easier to read once you know what it is context for.

The Council of Europe's language framework (Companion Volume, 2020) describes exactly this skill at two levels. At B1, a writer "Can compose basic e-mails/letters of a factual nature (e.g. to request information or to ask for and give confirmation)". At B2, they "Can use formality and conventions appropriate to the context when writing personal and professional letters and e-mails." Most professionals who write work email in English are already past the first. The first-line request is how the second sounds in practice: the convention in English business email is to ask early.

Self-studyGreetings and sign-offs are the frame, not the sentences3 min▶

Moving the request to the first line does not mean dropping the greeting. The Plain English Campaign's guide to writing business emails recommends opening with "Dear" and closing with "Best wishes" or "Regards". It says "Hi" is not really appropriate for business emails unless you are familiar with the person. And it adds a line many careful writers find reassuring: people are unlikely to be offended if you are too formal, but some may think you are being rude if you are too informal.

The same organisation's guide How to write in plain English, and the US federal plain language guidelines, argue hard against formal sentences: hidden verbs, passives, stock phrases. These two positions are often presented as a contradiction. They are about different layers of the email. The email guide is about the frame: the greeting and the sign-off. The plain English guidance is about the sentences inside the frame.

★ A formal frame around plain sentences · constructedDear Daniel, Could you approve the Q3 retention budget by Thursday? [two or three sentences of context] Best wishes, Mei Lin

Where the two sources genuinely differ, on whether erring formal is the safe side at all, the disagreement is real and is not settled here. A practical reading: with a new contact, a formal frame costs you nothing. Formality inside the sentences is what neither source defends. The same email guide also suggests keeping to a maximum of five paragraphs, which fits an email that leads with its request.

Real world

The preview pane. A regional operations lead reads most of her email on her phone between site visits. Her inbox shows the sender, the subject line and the first line of the body. Two emails arrive within a minute. One previews as "I hope this email finds you well. I am writing to..."; the other as "Could you confirm the Penang driver roster by 3pm today?" She answers the second from the car park. The first she flags for later, and later is Monday. Neither writer was less polite. One of them put the request where the reader could see it.

Part 3 · covers softeners that are not hedges

Keep the courtesy, drop the doubt

4 min live

Putting the request first can feel abrupt, and the instinct is to soften it. That instinct is right. The trouble is that two different kinds of word both feel soft. One kind keeps the request intact and makes it pleasant to receive. The other kind quietly takes the request back.

LiveSofteners keep the request; hedges take it back2 min▶

Here is the test. Remove the word. If the request is still there, and only a little less warm, the word was a softener. If the request becomes firmer, the word was a hedge: it was removing commitment, either yours or the reader's.

Softeners: keep themHedges: cut them from requests
please · thanks · thank youjust ("I just wanted to")
could you · would you · would you mindmaybe · perhaps · possibly
"when you have a moment today"I think · I'm not sure if
"I'd appreciate it if you could ... by Thursday"sorry to bother you · if possible

Look at "when you have a moment today". It is soft, and it still contains a deadline. Compare "at your earliest convenience", which sounds more polite and contains no deadline at all; session 1 counted it as an over-formal phrase. A good softener gives the reader room in how they do something, never in whether they do it.

One honest note on the checker you will use in the build-along. It counts "just" only inside phrases such as "just wanted to", "just checking" and "just a quick"; a bare "just" in the middle of a request slips past it. Your eye has to catch that one.

LiveWhen "please" is the wrong word2 min▶

The Plain English Campaign's guide How to write in plain English tells writers not to be afraid to give instructions, and suggests putting "please" in front of a command to soften it. Then it adds a nuance most style advice skips: "if something must be done, it is best not to say 'please' as it gives the reader the option to refuse the request."

So there are two kinds of email that need a reply, and they take different language.

★ A request (the reader can say no) · constructedCould you approve the Q3 retention budget by Thursday, please?
★ A requirement (the reader cannot say no) · constructedYou need to submit your Q3 timesheet by Friday 5pm. Payroll closes on Monday, and late timesheets move to the next pay run.

The second one has no "please" and is not rude. It is clear about what is required, by when and why. Adding "please could you possibly" to a payroll deadline does not make it kinder. It makes it sound negotiable, and the reader who treats it as negotiable is the one who gets paid late. Daniel can decline a budget, so Mei Lin asks him. A colleague cannot decline a payroll cut-off, so she tells them.

Self-studyWhat the session 4 bench counted2 min▶

The session 4 bench counts politeness markers ("please", "thanks", "could you", "would you", "appreciate" and a few others) separately, and never adds them to the flags. That design choice came from a finding, not a preference. On the five-rung ladder that rewrites Mei Lin's 119-word draft into a 55-word plain version, the draft had two politeness markers, and so did every rung down to the plain final. Five rewrites removed 22 flags and did not remove one "please" or "thanks".

The opposite lever is just as instructive. When the plain final was pushed back up into formal English, flags rose from 0 to 24 and the politeness markers fell from 2 to 0. More formal was not more polite on anything the bench can measure.

The real emails agree on which hedge to watch. Across the 400 real business emails in the bench, the most frequent hedge was "I think", 32 times; 18.5 percent of the emails contained at least one hedge, and 50.5 percent contained at least one politeness marker. Those writers were mostly native speakers in one US energy company in 2000 and 2001, so treat this as a picture of real email, not a standard of good email.

Real world

The deadline that read as a suggestion. A team lead in Manila, a constructed colleague of Mei Lin's, writes to her team: "Sorry to bother everyone, but maybe if possible could you just update the tracker by Friday? Thanks so much!" Half the team updates it on Monday. She was not unclear about the date. She wrapped the date in three signals that it was optional, and her team, sensibly, believed the signals. The next week she writes: "Please update the tracker by Friday 5pm; the client report is built from it on Monday. Thanks." Everyone does.

Build-along

Rewrite the email you need to send this week

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Five steps, in the order the reader meets them. Do the first pass on Mei Lin's email so you can see each step land, then do your own. If you did not bring one, write the email you have been putting off; everyone has one.

Read Mei Lin's email as Daniel would. Daniel Shaw is a constructed finance manager in London with a full inbox. Read the subject line and the first line only, then stop. Write down what you think she wants and by when. Most people cannot answer either question.

✗ As drafted · constructedSubject: Update Dear Daniel, I hope this email finds you well. I am writing to inform you that the analysis of the Q3 churn data has been completed. As per my last email, a decision on the retention budget will need to be made. Sorry to bother you, but could you maybe just approve it by Thursday if possible? Thanks and regards, Mei Lin
★ Step 1 · two questions, from the list and the first line onlyWhat does she want me to do? ______________ By when? ______________ If I cannot answer both, the subject line and line one are not doing their job.

Write the subject line. Action, then topic, then date. Then write your own underneath. If your email truly needs nothing from the reader, say so in the subject; that is also an action.

★ Step 2 · subject line template[Action needed] by [date]: [specific topic] Mei Lin: Approval needed by Thu: Q3 retention budget Yours: ________________ by ______: ________________

Write line one as the request. A question, starting "Could you" or "Can you", with the date inside it. Delete any opener that comes before it. Keep the greeting.

★ Step 3 · first line templateDear [name], Could you [verb] [the specific thing] by [day or date]? Mei Lin: Could you approve the Q3 retention budget by Thursday? Yours: Could you __________________________ by __________?

Add two or three sentences of context, then swap the softeners. The context answers "why now?" and "what do I need to look at?". Then go through every soft word with the test from Part 3: remove it, and see whether the request gets firmer. If it does, it was a hedge.

★ Step 4 · Mei Lin's rewrite · constructedSubject: Approval needed by Thu: Q3 retention budget Dear Daniel, Could you approve the Q3 retention budget by Thursday? We finished the Q3 churn analysis on Monday, and the slides are attached. Finance needs to sign off before 30 September, so Thursday leaves time to make the changes. If anything in the numbers needs a call, I am free on Wednesday afternoon. Thanks, Mei Lin

Notice what the checker missed. The seed opens with "I am writing to", and the checker does not count it: it is not on any of its lists. You know from Part 2 that it delays the request. That is why step 5 says argue with the rest. The checker counts the habits it can find; your eye is still the judge of the first line.

Homework

Before session 3

30 min
  1. Send the email you rewrote tonight. Note when the reply arrives and whether it answers the request in your first line. One email is not evidence, but it is a start.
  2. Rewrite your last five subject lines. Open your sent folder, copy the five most recent subject lines, and rewrite each as action, topic, date. Mark any that should have said "no action".
  3. Sort your soft words. Take two emails you sent this month and list every soft word in them. Put each in one of two columns: keeps courtesy, removes commitment. Keep the second column; it goes into your phrase bank in session 6.
  4. Find one must-do you phrased as a favour. Somewhere in your sent mail is a deadline written as "please could you possibly". Rewrite it the way the Plain English Campaign suggests for must-do items: what is needed, by when, and why, with no "please" in front of the requirement.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 2 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Mei Lin needs Daniel to approve a budget by Thursday. Which subject line does the most work in the inbox list?

The reader sorts the list before opening anything. Only B carries all three parts: the action the reader must take, a specific topic, and a date. C has a topic but no action; A and D have neither.

2 · In "Sorry to bother you, but could you maybe approve the budget by Thursday, please?", which words are softeners that should stay?

The test is to remove the word and see what happens to the request. Softeners only change the warmth; hedges change the commitment. On the session 4 ladder, every rewrite kept both politeness markers, and the plain final was no less polite than the draft.

3 · Timesheets must be in by Friday 5pm or staff are paid late. Why does the Plain English Campaign suggest leaving "please" out of this one?

The same guide suggests "please" in front of an ordinary instruction. The nuance is only for must-do items, where "please" turns a requirement into a request the reader may decline. D is false: the checker counts "please" as a politeness marker and never as a flag.
