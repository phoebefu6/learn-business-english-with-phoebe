Learn Business English with Phoebe · Session 1 of 6

Plain beats formal

Most people who learned English at school learned the letter-writing version of it: "I am writing to inform you", "please find attached herewith", "kindly revert at your earliest convenience". It feels safe, because it sounds like the documents you were taught to copy. To the person reading it, it sounds like a template, and a template hides the writer. Tonight you see why stiff English reads as unsure rather than respectful, where the stock phrases come from, and the six habits a plain-English check can actually count. You will mark them in a real draft by hand before any tool touches it.

Part 0

Where we start

You do not need more vocabulary to write good business English. You almost certainly have too much of the wrong kind: the formal kit that schools, templates and old company letters hand down. The six sessions take that kit apart one habit at a time, on one running email and on your own, and session 4 puts your writing next to 400 real business emails so the comparison is a count rather than somebody's taste.

LiveThe running case: Mei Lin's email2 min▶

Mei Lin Goh is a data analyst at Harbourline, a logistics company in Singapore. Both are constructed for teaching: she is not a real person and Harbourline is not a real company. Her working language is English, she speaks two other languages at home, and her analysis is excellent. Her emails get slow replies, and in her last review her manager wrote "communication could be more direct", which she found confusing, because she had been careful to be polite.

Here is the email she sent on Monday. Read it once as yourself, then once as a busy finance manager in London who has forty unread messages.

✗ As sent · 119 words, constructedDear all, I just wanted to kindly inform you that the analysis of the Q3 churn data has been completed by our team. Maybe perhaps we can have a discussion regarding the findings at your earliest convenience, as it is possible that a decision will need to be made on the retention budget prior to the end of the month and the approval of the finance team is also required before any changes can be implemented. Please find attached herewith the slides for your perusal. Sorry to bother you, I think it would be good if we could possibly meet this week. Kindly do the needful and revert back to me with your feedback. Thanks and regards, Mei Lin

Nothing in it is grammatically wrong. That is the point. It fails in a way a grammar checker cannot see.

Part 1 · covers why stiff reads as unsure

The reader does the cutting

6 min live

Every word in an email is either information or packaging. The reader has to read both to tell them apart. When the packaging is thick, the reader does the unpacking, and while they unpack they form an impression of the writer. The impression is rarely "how respectful". It is usually "what is she actually asking?"

LiveWhy "formal" reads as unsure, not as respect4 min▶

A reader cannot hear your tone. They infer it from the words, and they infer the most from the words that are not doing any work. Three readings are common, and none of them is the one the writer intended:

Packaging reads as distance. "Please be informed that" and "I wish to inform you" are phrases nobody says aloud to a colleague. A reader hears a form letter, and a form letter has nobody behind it. The UK Plain English Campaign's own test is to imagine you are talking to the reader across a table.

Hedges read as doubt. "I just wanted to", "maybe perhaps", "I think it would be good if we could possibly". Each one is meant as courtesy. Stacked, they tell the reader the writer is not sure the request is allowed, which invites the reader to decide it is not urgent. Ernest Gowers, writing for British civil servants in 1954, put the rule in one line: go straight to what you have to say, without precautionary words.

Hidden verbs and passives read as evasion. "A decision will need to be made" does not say who decides. "The approval of the finance team is also required" does not say who asks them. The US federal plain language guidelines name exactly this pair, hidden verbs and passive voice, as what gives writing "an officious and longwinded style".

Mei Lin intended all three as politeness. Her manager's review comment, "could be more direct", was the reader's experience of them.

Self-studyWhere the stock phrases come from3 min▶

Many of the phrases that feel most "proper" are survivals of old commercial letter-writing. In The Complete Plain Words (1954 edition, Her Majesty's Stationery Office), Gowers already warned officials against "the dry meaningless formulae of commercialese". Some, he wrote, needed no warning, because officials no longer wrote "your esteemed letter to hand" or addressed a correspondent as "your good self"; others, such as the "foolish begs" of "I beg to acknowledge", were still common. In British government writing they were already on the way out seventy years ago.

They did not die. They travelled, through school textbooks, letter-writing manuals, company templates and the emails of whoever trained you, and in some offices they are still the safe default. That history is the useful part: the phrases are not wrong, and you were not wrong to learn them. They are simply older than the readers you are now writing to, and those readers learned a different default.

One honest limit. It is often said that particular regional phrases, "do the needful" for example, descend directly from British colonial office English. That is plausible and widely repeated, but no source read for these pages establishes the line of descent, so it is not claimed here.

Real world

The polite email that waited a week. A request that opens "I just wanted to check whether it might perhaps be possible" gives the reader three separate signals that it can wait. A busy reader takes the hint. The same request as "Could you send the Q3 file by Friday?" is just as polite, because it asks rather than tells, and it can be answered in one line. Courtesy lives in "could you" and "thanks". It does not live in the hedges.

Part 2 · covers register

Plain is not casual, and it is not rude

5 min live

The fear behind stiff English is reasonable: if I drop the formal phrases, will I sound rude, or worse, careless? The answer is that register is a dial, not a ladder. Plain sits in the middle of it, and the goal is to choose your position on purpose.

LiveWhat plain English is not3 min▶

The Plain English Campaign's guide How to write in plain English opens by clearing away the myths, and the list is worth knowing because it answers the fears directly. Plain English is not baby talk. It does not mean shortening a message until it changes meaning. It is not about banning long words or letting grammar slip. And, in the guide's words, it is not as easy as people think.

What it is, in their definition: a message written with the reader in mind and with the right tone of voice, that is clear and concise. Tone of voice is in the definition. Plain English that sounds rude is bad plain English.

The same guide also clears some grammar myths that make non-native writers stiff: you may start a sentence with "and", "but", "because", "so" or "however", you may end one with a preposition, and you may split an infinitive. If a school rule is making a sentence harder to read, the rule is the problem.

Self-studyThe disagreement: is it safer to be too formal?3 min▶

Not every authority agrees, and it is worth seeing the disagreement rather than a tidy rule. The same Plain English Campaign that argues for plain English also publishes a short guide to business email which advises opening with "Dear" and closing with "Regards", says "Hi" is not really appropriate unless you know the person, and states that people are unlikely to be offended if you are too formal, while some may find you rude if you are too informal.

Both positions are reasonable, and they are about different things. The email guide is about the greeting and sign-off, the frame around the message. The plain English guide is about the sentences inside it. You can open with "Dear Ms Tan" and still write "Could you send the file by Friday?" underneath. What neither guide recommends is formality inside the sentences: no plain English authority read for these pages defends "please find attached herewith".

The Council of Europe's language framework puts the skill in exactly these terms. At B2, its description of sociolinguistic appropriateness includes expressing yourself "confidently, clearly and politely in a formal or informal register, appropriate to the situation and person(s) concerned". The skill is the choosing, not a fixed level of formality.

Real world

Two offices, one thread. A colleague in Manila writes "Noted with thanks, will revert." A colleague in Frankfurt reads "revert" as "go back to a previous state" and wonders what is being undone. Neither of them made an error in their own office's English. The thread failed in the gap between them, which is why session 4 flags regional idioms as "may confuse global readers" and never as wrong.

Part 3 · covers the six countable habits

Six habits you can count

4 min live

"Be more direct" is advice nobody can act on. Six specific habits are what make a draft read as stiff, and every one of them can be found by looking for phrases. That matters, because a habit you can count is a habit you can watch change.

HabitWhat to look forWho says so
Hedges"just wanted to", "maybe", "perhaps", "I think", "possibly", "sorry to bother"Gowers: no "precautionary words" before the point
Hidden verbsmake a decision, have a discussion, carry out a review, the approval ofplainlanguage.gov; Plain English Campaign ("nominalizations")
Passive voice"was completed", "is required", "will be made", with no person doing itPlain English Campaign: aim for 80 to 90 percent active verbs
Long sentencesmore than one idea, joined by "as", "and", "which"Plain English Campaign: an average of 15 to 20 words

Self-studyWhat the six are not: structure and the ask2 min▶

All six are habits inside sentences. Whether your email has a clear ask, where the ask sits, and how to deliver bad news are questions of structure, and they are taught properly next door in Communication ↗, which has its own clarity scorer for exactly that. The two work together: structure decides what the reader meets first, the six habits decide how hard each sentence is to read once they meet it.

A note on the target. The Council of Europe's framework states that its top level, C2, "has no relation whatsoever" with the performance of an idealised native speaker, and its redeveloped pronunciation scale moved the focus from accent to intelligibility. That is the standard here too. The aim is English a busy reader in any office understands on the first pass, not English that sounds like it came from London or New York.

Build-along

Mark six habits by hand, then check

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Marking by hand first matters. A tool that finds a habit for you teaches you to trust the tool. Finding it yourself, then checking, teaches your eye, and your eye is what you will have when you are writing fast at 6pm. Use Mei Lin's email for the first pass, then one of your own.

Read Mei Lin's email aloud, once. Out loud, at normal speed. Put a small dot wherever you would never say those words to a colleague across a desk. Most people put the first dot in the first line.

Homework

Before session 2

30 min
  1. Mark three more of your sent emails by hand, then check them. Keep a tally of the six habits across all four emails. The tally, not any single email, tells you your pattern.
  2. Collect your stock phrases. Copy every phrase from your four emails that you would not say aloud. This list is the start of the personal phrase bank you build in session 6.
  3. Find one email you received that you answered fast. Mark it the same way. Most people find it has fewer marks than their own, and that it is no less polite.
  4. Bring one real email you need to send this week. Session 2 rewrites it, subject line first.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Mei Lin's manager wrote "communication could be more direct". Which change answers that comment without making her rude?

Politeness markers are not on the list of six habits. What reads as indirect is the precautionary wording around the request: hedges, hidden verbs, passives and stock phrases. Remove those and the request is direct and still polite.

2 · The Plain English Campaign's email guide says people are unlikely to be offended if you are too formal. Does that contradict its plain English guide?

The two guides address different layers. A formal frame is a safe default with a new contact; formality inside the sentences is what neither guide defends. D is false: the campaign lists phrases like these as ones to replace.

3 · A colleague writes "Noted with thanks, will revert." Why does session 4 flag "will revert" as a regional idiom rather than as an error?

The target is English a reader in any office understands on the first pass. A regional idiom is correct where it is used and a risk where it is not, which is why the bench says "may confuse global readers" and never "wrong".
