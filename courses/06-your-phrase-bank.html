Your phrase bank and practice loop

A generic list of "phrases to avoid" fixes somebody else's habits. Your own sent folder holds yours, already sorted by how often you reach for them. Part 1 · covers mining your sent mail

Your sent folder is the textbook

6 min live

Everybody has a small set of phrases they reach for under time pressure, and they are remarkably stable. Twenty sent emails are usually enough to find yours. The detectors find the candidates; you decide which are habits and which were the right words that day.

LiveWhy your own mail beats a generic list3 min▶

Generic "phrases to avoid" lists, including the Plain English Campaign's own words-to-avoid list and the long substitution table in the US plain language guidelines, are good references. They are also long, and most of their entries are phrases you never write. The phrases you do write are a short list, and you reach for them under the same conditions every time: a request to someone senior, a reply you are late with, a message to an office you do not know.

That is why the bank is built from your sent folder rather than from a book. Each row is a phrase you actually used, so each swap is one you will actually get to make, probably this week.

Self-studyWhat goes in the bank, and what stays out3 min▶

In: a phrase the detectors flagged that you would not say across a table, with the plain version you will use instead. A hidden verb with its verb ("make a decision" to "decide"). A regional idiom with a version a reader in any office understands, and a note on which colleagues it is fine with.

Out: politeness markers, which are never flags. Passives where the doer does not matter ("the slides are attached"). The single honest "I think" where you really are unsure: 32 of the 400 real Enron emails on the bench used "I think", and the habit is the stack, not the one. Overruling the detector is part of keeping the bank, and every overrule gets one line of reason so that next month you remember why.

Real world

Three phrases, most of the hits. When Mei Lin, the constructed analyst from session 1, mined twenty of her emails, three phrases made up most of her flags: "kindly", "I just wanted to" and "please find attached". She had expected her grammar to be the problem. It was three phrases, each with a ready replacement, and the bank had eleven rows by the end of the evening.

Part 2 · covers the monthly re-run

Watch one number, and stop at the real median

5 min live

Session 4's homework gave you a baseline: the median flags per 100 words of five of your emails. Once a month, run five new ones and write the median down with the date. One number, twelve times a year, is enough to see whether the bank is working.

LiveWhy the target is the real median, not zero3 min▶

On the bench, 168 of the 400 real emails (42 percent) score zero and 58 percent carry at least one flag. The median is 1.11 flags per 100 words. Real business email, written by people under time pressure, is not flag-free, and it does not need to be.

Once a number becomes the target, it is tempting to write for the number. The bench's lists are finite, so a writer can avoid every listed phrase and still be stiff: "I would be most grateful for your earliest attention to the below" contains no listed phrase and reads like a form letter. That is the reason every hit on the bench is shown by name, and the reason the bank has an "overruled" column. The number tells you which way you are moving. The reader tells you whether you arrived.

Self-studyWhat to do when the number stops falling2 min▶

A plateau usually means one of two things. Either your remaining hits are honest ones you would defend, in which case you are done with this number and the useful work moves to meetings and spoken updates, or one detector is holding most of your hits month after month. Look at the named hits, not the total: a plateau made of six passives is a different problem from a plateau made of six hedges, and session 1's table names the fix for each.

If you write for readers in several regions, keep a separate eye on the regional detector. The Enron base is one American company and holds zero regional idioms, so it cannot tell you which ones your readers know. Only your readers can.

Part 3 · covers a practice loop small enough to keep

Minutes a day, not evenings a week

4 min live

A practice plan that needs a free evening fails in the first busy week. The loop below costs about thirty seconds a day, ten minutes a week and half an hour a month, and each ring produces one thing you can see.

LiveThe four rings, and what each produces3 min▶

Daily: read it aloud before you send. Session 1's across-the-table test, on every email that asks for something. Thirty seconds. Anything you would not say to the person gets cut or swapped from the bank.

Weekly: one meeting line. Before the meeting you care most about, write one line from session 3, an entry line or a disagreement in four parts, and say it aloud three times. Ten minutes.

Monthly: five emails on the bench. The median goes in your log with the date; new hits go in the bank. Half an hour.

Quarterly: the can-do check. The Council of Europe's descriptors are written as things a person can do, which makes them a usable self-check. For register, B2 reads "Can express themselves confidently, clearly and politely in a formal or informal register, appropriate to the situation and person(s) concerned", and C1 "Can adjust their level of formality (register and style) to suit the social context". For meetings, C1 includes "Can make critical remarks or express disagreement diplomatically." Mark a descriptor only when you can name a moment this quarter when you did it.

Self-studyAn honest limit on self-assessment2 min▶

The CEFR descriptors are designed for teaching and assessment, and a self-check against them is a reflection tool, not a certified level. If you need a level for a job or a visa, that is a formal test, which is outside these pages. The point of the quarterly check is narrower: to notice that "can make critical remarks diplomatically" has moved from something you avoid to something you did on Tuesday.

Real world

The loop that survived a quarter-end. In the last week of a quarter, nobody runs a bench or prepares meeting lines. The daily ring still runs, because it is thirty seconds inside an action that was happening anyway: sending the email. Plans built only from the outer rings stop in the first busy week. Plans that start from the inner ring survive it.

Part 4 · the final scorecard

Eight checks on what the six sessions built

4 min live

Eight things that are either true of your English at work today or not yet. Score what you do, not what you intend to do. Each row names the session to go back to.

I have marked at least four of my own emails by hand for the six habits, and I know my three most frequent ones. Session 1. The habits are hedges, hidden verbs, passives, long sentences, over-formal phrases and regional idioms; politeness markers are never on the list.
My subject lines name the action and the date when I need something, and none of them is just "Update" or "Following up". Session 2. The Plain English Campaign's email guide: title your email clearly in the subject box, and keep titles short.
When I ask for something, the request is in the first two lines, as a question with a date, and the courtesy is in "could you" and "thanks" rather than in hedges. Session 2. On the bench ladder, five rewrites removed 22 flags and kept both politeness markers on every rung.
I have an entry line, a four-part disagreement and a clarifying question ready for my next important meeting, and I have said them aloud. Session 3. The CEFR describes C1 as able to "make critical remarks or express disagreement diplomatically"; the phrase bank is how you get there.
I have a dated baseline: the median flags per 100 words of five of my emails, next to the real median of 1.11. Session 4. 400 real Enron emails, computed in the browser; the real 90th percentile is 3.03.
When I give a number aloud, I say the important one twice in different words, and I never leave "points" and "percent" for the room to guess. Session 5. A room hears a number once.
My phrase bank has at least ten rows from my own sent mail, each with the plain version, and the overruled hits carry one line of reason. Session 6. Sorted by how often I reach for each phrase.
I read every request email aloud before I send it, and I have done it on at least five working days in a row. Session 6. The daily ring is thirty seconds inside an action that was happening anyway, which is why it survives a busy week.
Real world

Where Mei Lin landed, constructed. At her next review, the comment "could be more direct" was gone. Her emails were not shorter because she had learned new grammar; they were shorter because three phrases had left them and a request had moved to the first line. She scored 13 of 16 on the check. The three missing points were the meeting lines, and that is where her next quarter went.

Build-along

Build your phrase bank from twenty sent emails

★ 22 min · everyone builds

You need twenty emails you sent in the last month, ideally ones that asked for something, and a document or spreadsheet with three columns. The bench in session 4 does the counting; the checker below does the same for one email at a time.

Collect twenty sent emails. From the last month, ones that asked for something if you can. Copy each body only: no signatures, no quoted replies underneath, nothing confidential you would not paste anywhere.

★ Step 1 · what to copykeep: the text you typed, greeting to sign-off drop: your signature block drop: the quoted thread underneath ("On Monday ... wrote:") drop: anything confidential; replace names and numbers with X

Run each one through the checker or the bench. Note the flags per 100 words and copy every named hit into a tally, one line per phrase, adding a mark each time it appears again.

★ Step 2 · the tallyphrase detector count kindly revert regional ||||| |||| at your earliest convenience over-formal ||||| | the approval of hidden verb |||| ... ... ...

Sort and write the swaps. Most frequent first. For each, write the version you would say across a table. Use the Plain English Campaign's words to avoid and the US guidelines' simple-words table when you are stuck; they list replacements such as "prior to" to "before" and "commence" to "start".

★ Step 3 · the bank rowsI write I will write instead kindly revert please reply by <day> at your earliest convenience by <day> the approval of approve I just wanted to (delete; start at the verb) prior to before

Overrule what you would defend. Any hit you would keep goes in the third column with one line of reason. Typical keeps: a passive where the doer does not matter, one honest "I think", a regional phrase in a thread where every reader uses it.

★ Step 4 · overruled, with a reasonthe slides are attached kept: nobody cares who attached them I think kept: I genuinely do not know yet noted with thanks kept with the Manila team only: they all use it

Write the baseline and book the re-run. Your median flags per 100 words across the twenty, today's date, and the date of the next monthly run in your calendar.

★ Step 5 · the log linedate: ____________ emails: 20 median flags per 100 words: ____ real median 1.11 · real 90th percentile 3.03 (session 4) top three habits: ____________ · ____________ · ____________ next re-run: ____________ (in the calendar, with a reminder)
Homework

The next four weeks

30 min
  1. Run the daily ring for five working days in a row and note how many emails changed after the read-aloud.
  2. Add three rows to the bank from emails you send this week, including one overrule with its reason.
  3. Prepare one meeting line a week for four weeks, and keep the four lines with the bank.
  4. Do the first monthly re-run on five new emails, write the median beside the baseline, and re-score the final check.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 6 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Why build the phrase bank from your own sent mail rather than from a published list of phrases to avoid?

The published lists are good references, and step 3 uses them for replacements. The bank is built from your mail because the habits are personal and stable, and a short personal list gets used.

2 · Your monthly median has reached 0.9 flags per 100 words. Should you push it to zero?

Chasing zero invites writing around the detectors' finite lists. The number shows direction; the reader decides arrival. The overruled column is how the bank keeps the number honest.

3 · Which ring of the practice loop is most likely to survive a busy quarter-end, and why?

Plans built only from the outer rings stop in the first busy week. The inner ring costs almost nothing and runs every day, which is why it is the terracotta box on the scorecard.
