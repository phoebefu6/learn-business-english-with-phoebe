Numbers and updates, out loud

In an email, a reader can look at a number twice. In a call, they hear it once, at your speed, while they are still thinking about the last thing you said. Most of the confusion in a spoken update is not grammar. It is a number that three people heard three different ways, a change described with the wrong small word, or "maybe around possibly 4" where a range would have done the job. Tonight is the English of numbers said aloud: how to say them so they land, how to describe change and uncertainty precisely, and the signpost phrases that turn a 90-second update into something a room can follow.

Part 0

Where we are

Sessions 1 to 4 were mostly about writing, where the reader controls the pace. Tonight the listener does not. The six habits still matter, because a hedge or a 40-word sentence is even harder to follow by ear, but two new things now decide whether you are understood: how you say the numbers, and whether the room knows where you are in the update. Both are language you can practise, and both are small.

Part 1 · covers saying numbers

A room hears a number once

6 min live

A written number waits for the reader. A spoken one is gone by the time the next word arrives, and each listener keeps whatever piece of it they caught. The fix is not to speak more slowly. It is to say the number in a shape that survives one hearing: the level, the change, and the size of the change, in plain words, with the important one said twice.

LiveThe running case: Mei Lin's Monday update2 min▶

Mei Lin Goh is a data analyst at Harbourline, a logistics company in Singapore. Both are constructed for teaching, and so is every number on this page: no figure below was measured anywhere. Every Monday she gives a 90-second churn update in a team call that includes her manager in Singapore and a finance lead in London, also constructed.

This week's news is good. Monthly churn, the share of customers who cancel in a month, fell from 4.2 percent in August to 3.9 percent in September. Last week she said "churn is down point three", moved on, and spent the next ten minutes answering three different questions about what she meant. Nothing she said was wrong. The room simply heard one number and kept three different parts of it.

LiveSay it so it lands the first time2 min▶

Decimals, large numbers and percentages each have a spoken form, and the spoken form is not always the one your eye reads. Three rules cover most of the trouble:

Say the digits after the point one by one. 3.9 is "three point nine". 3.25 is "three point two five", never "three point twenty-five". For a number below one, say the zero: 0.3 is "zero point three" (in British English also "nought point three"). "Point three" on its own is correct, and it is exactly what Listener 1 lost.

Give large numbers their unit word. 18,500 is "eighteen thousand five hundred". 1,250,000 is "one point two five million", or, if precision does not matter, "about one and a quarter million". A string of digits ("one two five zero zero zero zero") is how people read phone numbers, not quantities.

Always say the unit. "Three point nine" is a number. "Three point nine percent of customers" is a fact. When the listener has to supply the unit, each listener supplies a different one.

WrittenSayAvoid
3.9%"three point nine percent""three point nine" with no unit
0.3 pp"zero point three percentage points", or "a third of a point""point three", "zero point three percent"
18,500"eighteen thousand five hundred""one eight five zero zero"
3.9% of 8,000"about four in every hundred customers, 312 accounts"the percentage alone, when people think in accounts

The last row matters for a mixed room. A finance lead may think in percentages, an operations manager in accounts. "About four in every hundred" is a picture; "312 accounts" is a count. Both are the same 3.9 percent of a constructed 8,000-account base.

LivePercentage points versus percent: the one that changes the claim2 min▶

When a rate changes, there are two honest ways to describe the change, and they produce different numbers:

In percentage points, you subtract: 4.2 minus 3.9 is 0.3 percentage points. This is the absolute change in the rate.

In percent, you compare the change with where you started: 0.3 divided by 4.2 is 0.071, so churn fell by about 7 percent. This is the relative change.

Both are true. What is not true is "churn fell by 0.3 percent". That sentence claims churn went from 4.2 to about 4.19, a change nobody would report. It is the commonest numbers slip in spoken English at work, and it comes from saying "percent" where you meant "points". In speech you can shorten "percentage points" to "points" once the rate is clear: "down 0.3 points".

The safe habit: say the level, then name the kind of change. "From 4.2 to 3.9 percent. That is 0.3 points, about 7 percent." Nobody can mishear that, and nobody has to calculate.

Self-studyRounding out loud, and saying it twice3 min▶

Round for the ear, keep the precise figure for the slide or the email. 0.3 divided by 4.2 is 7.14 percent. Said aloud, "seven point one four percent" adds three syllables and no information, so say "about 7 percent". "About", "roughly" and "just under" are not hedges here: they tell the listener exactly what you did, which is round. They are not on the hedge list the session 4 bench uses, and they should not be.

Round in the honest direction. 7.1 is "about 7". 6.6 is "about 7" too, but "just under 7" is better, because it tells the listener which side of the round number you are on. When a target is involved ("we need to be under 4 percent"), never round across it: 3.96 is "just under 4", not "4".

Say the important number twice, in different words. Repetition in the same words sounds like you lost your place. Repetition in different words gives a second listener a second way in: "3.9 percent, about four in every hundred customers"; "down 0.3 points, about 7 percent"; "24 fewer accounts than August, out of roughly 8,000". The first version serves the people who think in rates, the second the people who think in things.

The Plain English Campaign recommends an average sentence length of 15 to 20 words for writing. A spoken sentence that carries a number should usually sit at the short end of that, because the listener is holding the number in their head while the rest of the sentence arrives.

Real world

The 0.3 that became a budget question. A constructed scene, and a common one. An analyst says "conversion is up point five" in a quarterly call. The sales director hears half a percent and shrugs. The CFO hears half a percentage point on a 2 percent base, a quarter more conversions, and asks why nobody flagged it. Both heard correctly. One sentence, "from 2 to 2.5 percent, half a point, about a quarter more customers converting", would have given them the same picture before anybody spoke. All figures here are constructed.

Part 2 · covers change and uncertainty

Rose, fell, held steady, and how sure you are

5 min live

An update is mostly about change, and English has a small, precise kit for it: a verb that says which way and how far, and three prepositions that say what the number refers to. Uncertainty has a precise kit too. A range with a best guess is information a listener can plan with. A stack of hedges is information only about your nerves.

LiveVerbs of change, and the three small words3 min▶

Pick the verb for the direction and the size, then let three prepositions carry the numbers. Each verb below is plain and safe in any office.

VerbUse it forExample (constructed)
rose, increased, went upany upward change"Late deliveries rose from 2 to 3 percent."
fell, decreased, went downany downward change"Churn fell from 4.2 to 3.9 percent."
edged up, edged downa small change, and you want it heard as small"Costs edged up by 1 percent."
held steady, was flatno meaningful change"Volume held steady at about 18,500 parcels a day."
doubled, halvedexactly or almost exactly 2x or 0.5x; say "almost" if it is not"Complaints halved, from 60 to 30."

From is where you started, to is where you are, by is the size of the change. "Fell to 3.9 percent" and "fell by 3.9 percent" are different claims, and a listener cannot see the preposition you meant. When the number matters, say all three: "fell from 4.2 to 3.9 percent, by 0.3 points".

Two cautions. Keep dramatic verbs (soared, collapsed, plunged) for changes that deserve them; in a status update they sound like opinion. And "doubled" is a strong word: it invites somebody to check the arithmetic, so be sure before you say it.

LiveUncertainty as information, not as hedging2 min▶

Mei Lin's September figure is not final: a few late cancellations still arrive in the first week of October. Last week she covered that with "maybe around possibly 4, I think". This week she says:

★ The uncertainty, said as information · constructedSeptember churn is between 3.8 and 4.1 percent, most likely 3.9. It moves a little until late cancellations close on the 10th. I'll send the final figure that afternoon.

That is three things a listener can use: the range, the best guess, and when the uncertainty ends. "Maybe around possibly 4" contains none of them. It sounds more modest and gives the listener less.

Session 1 called hedges "precautionary words", Gowers's phrase from 1954. A range is the opposite of a precautionary word: it is the precaution itself, stated as a fact. If you are not sure, say how not sure, in numbers.

Self-studyOne honest hedge is not the habit2 min▶

None of this means a speaker may never say "I think". In the 400 real business emails behind the session 4 bench, the commonest hedge is exactly "I think": it appears 32 times. 18.5 percent of those emails contain at least one hedge, and they are ordinary, working emails.

The difference is density and position. "I think the September number will hold" is one honest judgement, clearly labelled as a judgement, after the facts. "I think maybe it is possibly around 4" is four hedges standing in front of a number, and the listener remembers the hedges rather than the number. The session 4 bench counts "I think", "maybe" and "possibly" on the hedge list because it reads written text; in speech the rule of thumb is the same. One hedge that marks an opinion as an opinion is honest. A stack in front of a fact is the habit.

A useful pair for meetings: say the number as a fact or a range, then, if you have a view, give it its own sentence. "Between 3.8 and 4.1, most likely 3.9. I think it will land at 3.9, because late cancellations have been small all year."

Real world

The forecast nobody could plan with. A constructed scene. An operations lead asks how many parcels to staff for next week. The analyst says "maybe around twenty thousand, possibly more, I'm not sure". The lead staffs for twenty thousand and is short on Thursday. The same uncertainty, said as "between 19,000 and 22,000, most likely 20,000, and Thursday is the risky day", lets the lead put two extra people on Thursday. The analyst knew exactly as much both times. Only the second sentence handed that knowledge over. Figures constructed.

Part 3 · covers the spoken status update

Tell the room where you are

4 min live

A reader can see that an email has three paragraphs. A listener cannot see anything. Signpost phrases do the job that headings and paragraph breaks do on a page: they say how many things are coming and which one you are on. They are the cheapest language upgrade in a spoken update, and they cost about two seconds each.

LiveSignpost phrases that work in any office2 min▶

A signpost is a short phrase that names what is coming or where you are. It carries no content of its own, which is exactly why it helps: the listener spends nothing on it and gains a map.

JobPhrases
Announce the shape"Three things: what changed, what is next, and what I need." · "Two updates and one request."
Mark each block"First, ..." · "Second, ..." · "And third, ..." · "On what is next: ..."
Flag the headline"The headline is ..." · "The one number to remember is ..."
Hand over the request"What I need from you is ..." · "Could you ... by Thursday?"
Close"So: churn is down, the final figure comes on the 10th, and I need the budget by Thursday." · "That's all from me."

Number the blocks out loud even when it feels obvious. "First" and "second" sound plain to you because you wrote the script. To a listener in London at 5pm on a poor line, they are the difference between following and catching up.

LiveWhat the CEFR says a clear speaker does1 min▶

The Council of Europe's Companion Volume (2020) has a scale called Addressing audiences, and its descriptors read like a checklist for a spoken update. At B1, a speaker's presentation is "clear enough to be followed without difficulty most of the time", and they "Can take follow-up questions, but may have to ask for repetition if the delivery is rapid". At B2, the presentation is "clear, systematically developed", "with highlighting of significant points".

Read those as language targets, not as grades. "Systematically developed" is what signposts give you. "Highlighting of significant points" is saying the important number twice. And the B1 note about asking for repetition is worth turning round: if you are the speaker, saying the key number twice is how you spare your listeners from having to ask.

Self-studyWhat this Part does not teach: delivery and structure1 min▶

Everything above is about words: which phrases, which verbs, which small words around a number. Two neighbouring skills are deliberately left to the places that teach them properly.

Delivery, meaning pace, voice, pauses, slides and nerves, lives in Public Speaking ↗. Structure, meaning what goes first, where the ask sits, and how to give bad news, lives in Communication ↗. The three-block shape on this page is used as a frame to practise the language inside it, not as a theory of how to structure a message.

Real world

The update that was all in the last ten seconds. A constructed scene. A project manager talks for two minutes about vendor delays, testing, and a holiday, then says "so, yeah, we might need sign-off on the extra week". Half the call has stopped listening by then. The same content, opened with "Two things and one request: the delay, the new date, and a sign-off I need today", gets the sign-off in the call. Nothing about the facts changed. The listeners were told what to listen for.

Build-along

Script and say your 90-second update

★ 22 min · everyone builds

You will write a spoken update about your own work and read it aloud against a timer. Writing a script for 90 seconds of speech can feel excessive. It is not: the script is where you choose the number's two forms and the range before you are under pressure, and after three or four weeks you stop needing it, because the shape has become a habit.

Pick one number from your week, and write it two ways. One number only: the one your manager would ask about first. Write it as a level and as a change, or as points and as percent, or as an absolute count and as a relative change. Check the arithmetic now, on paper, so you never do it out loud.

★ Step 1 · one number, two waysThe number: ______________________ (what it measures) Last period: ______ This period: ______ Way 1 (level and change in points): "from ____ to ____, that is ____ points" Way 2 (relative, or a count): "about ____ percent" / "____ fewer ____" Mei Lin, constructed: "from 4.2 to 3.9 percent, 0.3 points, about 7 percent"

Add one range, if anything is still moving. If your number is final, skip this. If it is not, write the range, your best guess, and when the uncertainty ends. Then read the sentence and delete any "maybe", "possibly" or "I think" that crept in front of it.

★ Step 2 · the range, said as information"Between ____ and ____, most likely ____." "It moves until ______________; I'll send the final figure on ______." Delete if present in front of the number: maybe · possibly · around possibly · I think

Write three signposted blocks, one or two sentences each. Start with Mei Lin's constructed script below, read it aloud once so you hear the shape, then fill the template with your own work. Keep every sentence short enough to say in one breath.

★ Step 3a · Mei Lin's script, constructedThree things: what changed, what is next, and what I need from you. First, what changed. Churn fell from 4.2 to 3.9 percent in September. That is 0.3 points, about 7 percent fewer customers leaving. Second, what is next. September is not final yet: it is between 3.8 and 4.1, most likely 3.9. Late cancellations close on the 10th, and I'll send the final figure that afternoon. Third, what I need. Could you confirm the retention budget by Thursday, so the offer goes out next week? So: churn is down, the final number comes on the 10th, and I need the budget by Thursday. That's all from me.
★ Step 3b · your script, fill it inThree things: ______________, ______________, and ______________. First, ______________. [your number, way 1] That is ______________. [your number, way 2] Second, ______________. [what is next; your range if you have one] Third, what I need. Could you ______________ by ______________? So: ______________, ______________, and ______________. That's all from me.

Read it aloud against a 90-second timer. Stand up if you can, and read at the speed you would use in the call, not faster. Mark every place you stumbled or ran out of breath: those are the sentences to shorten. Then read it a second time. If it runs over 90 seconds, cut words from block two, never the number or the request.

★ Step 4 · the timed readRead 1: ____ seconds stumbled at: ______________________ Read 2: ____ seconds stumbled at: ______________________ Did I say the unit every time? yes / no Did I say "points" where I meant points? yes / no Did every block open with its signpost? yes / no

Optional: check the written script. Paste your script into the checker below. It runs the same six detectors as the session 4 bench, built for written text, so treat it as a second pair of eyes. If it flags a hedge in front of your number, cut it. If it flags "I think" in a separate sentence that labels your opinion as an opinion, you may decide to keep it.

★ Step 5 · what to look for in the checkhedges in front of a number -> cut them; the range does that job long sentences -> split them; you have to say them in one breath hidden verbs and passives -> "a decision is needed" becomes "could you decide" "about" and "roughly" -> not on the hedge list; they say you rounded

The checker reads text; your listeners hear speech. It cannot hear whether you said "points" or "percent", whether you paused after "First", or whether you said the number twice. Those are the parts of tonight that only the timed read tests. Use the checker for the sentences, and your own ears for the rest.

Homework

Before session 6

30 min
  1. Give your 90-second update for real in your next team call, from the script. Afterwards, write down any question someone asked about a number. Each one is a place where your two ways of saying it did not yet cover what they needed.
  2. Collect five number sentences from your own email or slides. Rewrite each one for the ear: unit said, decimals said digit by digit, change named as points or percent, the important one said twice.
  3. Listen for "percent" in one meeting you attend. Each time someone describes a change in a rate, note whether they meant points or percent, and whether the room could tell. Most people find at least one case nobody questioned.
  4. Start a page called "numbers and updates" in your phrase bank. Copy in the signposts and verbs of change you actually used this week. Session 6 builds the full phrase bank, and this page is one of its sections.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 5 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Churn fell from 4.2 percent to 3.9 percent (constructed figures). Which sentence describes the change correctly?

4.2 minus 3.9 is 0.3 percentage points, the absolute change. 0.3 divided by 4.2 is about 7 percent, the relative change. A would mean churn moved from 4.2 to about 4.19; C mixes the two kinds of change; D confuses "to" with "by".

2 · Mei Lin's September figure is not final. Which sentence gives her listeners the most they can act on?

A range, a best guess and the date the uncertainty ends are all information. A is a hedge stack that says only that she is unsure. B hides real uncertainty. C withholds what she does know. Being honest about uncertainty means saying how big it is.

3 · Why open a spoken update with "Three things: what changed, what is next, and what I need"?

Signposts do for a listener what headings do for a reader. The CEFR's B2 description of addressing audiences asks for a presentation that is "systematically developed", "with highlighting of significant points", and signposts are the language that does that. Formality and nerves are not the reason, and there is nothing special about three.
